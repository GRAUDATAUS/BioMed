The Pattern

This is not a one-time problem. It is how the model layer works.

When you send research to a frontier model, you do not control what happens next. The vendor does. And the vendor changes the rules on its own schedule, for its own reasons, often disclosed only in documents few people read.

  • Retention policies change. Data you were told would not be kept becomes data that is kept for a fixed window, with limited recourse.
  • Routing changes. The model you selected can be swapped for another based on what your prompt or workspace contains, with security and biology content among the triggers.
  • Capabilities change. A model can be tuned to give weaker answers on certain topics, sometimes without a clear notice to the user.
  • The license itself changes. End-user terms are revised unilaterally. A new model class can ship outside the scope of the protections you negotiated. Your practical choice is to accept the new terms or stop using the product.

None of this requires bad faith on the vendor's part. It is the structural reality of renting access to someone else's model: they hold the controls, and the controls move. The most recent release simply makes the pattern impossible to ignore.

The Live Example

June 2026: Anthropic's Mythos-class release.

The clearest illustration arrived with Anthropic's new Mythos-class models, including Claude Fable 5. In a matter of days, it demonstrated every part of the pattern at once.

  • Mandatory retention that cannot be turned off. These models carry a 30-day retention window for prompts and outputs, and it applies even to organizations that previously operated under Zero Data Retention agreements. ZDR does not extend to this model class.
  • Content-based rerouting. Requests flagged by safety classifiers for cybersecurity or biology content are re-run on a different model. The flag can fire on benign material, and even on a workspace's existing files before the user types anything unusual.
  • Disclosed capability limits. A passage in the model's system card described responses being quietly degraded for certain advanced work. The disclosure drew immediate criticism, and the provider walked the behavior back.
  • The market reacted within a day. At least one major enterprise restricted internal use while its legal teams reviewed the retention implications.

For a research institution, two words in that list are decisive: biology content. The exact subject matter of biomedical science is a documented trigger for routing decisions you do not control and were not consulted on. And whatever was negotiated in a prior agreement did not prevent any of it.

The Real Issue

The problem is control, not any single policy.

Retention, routing, and capability limits are symptoms. The underlying condition is that control sits with the vendor. A protection that depends on a vendor's current policy is only as durable as the vendor's next decision.

That is why the answer cannot be a better contract or a stronger assurance. Assurances can be revised. The only durable form of data sovereignty is one that does not depend on what the vendor does next, because the sensitive data never leaves your control in identifiable form to begin with.

Sovereignty has to be a property of the architecture, not a clause in a contract.
